Yes—but **the exact question you are asking is less studied than EOT-style robust attacks**, and I think that distinction matters.

Your question is:

> Take a perturbation optimized for **one specific observation** (x). Do not optimize it over transformations. Now transform the underlying image and ask whether the *same perturbation, transformed along with the object*, remains adversarial?

Formally:

[
x_{\rm adv}=x+\delta
]

then

[
T(x_{\rm adv})=T(x)+T(\delta),
]

and ask whether

[
f(T(x)+T(\delta))\ne f(T(x)).
]

That is different from:

[
\delta^*=
\arg\max_\delta
E_T[L(f(T(x+\delta)),y)],
]

which is EOT.

### What I found

The closest foundational result is actually quite revealing. Athalye et al.'s work showed that **ordinary adversarial examples can fail under tiny transformations**: their example could lose its adversarial property after a very small zoom. They then deliberately optimized perturbations over transformations to produce transformation-invariant attacks. ([OpenAI][1])

So the literature has extensively studied:

> **"How do I make an attack survive transformations?"**

and a large literature subsequently uses input transformations to improve **adversarial transferability**. For example, scale-invariant, translation-invariant, diverse-input, and newer dynamic-transformation attacks deliberately optimize with transformed inputs. ([ScienceDirect][2])

But that's **not quite your question**.

---

### Your question is more like a *natural equivariance test for adversarial vulnerability*

You would generate **one ordinary attack**:

[
\delta_x = A(f,x)
]

and then measure:

[
S(T)
====

\mathbf 1[
f(T(x)+T(\delta_x))
\neq
f(T(x))
].
]

Then sweep through a physically meaningful transformation space:

[
T_\theta,\qquad
\theta\in G.
]

For iLab, you could have:

[
\theta =
(\text{rotation},\text{scale},\text{viewpoint},\ldots).
]

Then measure something like

[
\boxed{
R(x,\delta)
===========

\mathbb E_{T\sim G}
[
S(T)
]
}
]

**without having optimized (\delta) for (G).**

That is a very different measurement from EOT.

---

### And iLab-20M makes this unusually clean

This is where I think your idea gets interesting.

You have the same physical object under controlled transformations:

[
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n.
]

Generate an attack on **only (x_1)**:

[
x_1+\delta_1.
]

Then transform the *attack itself* according to the known physical transformation:

[
T_{1\rightarrow j}(\delta_1).
]

Now test:

[
x_j+T_{1\rightarrow j}(\delta_1).
]

You can ask:

[
\boxed{
\text{How far does adversarial vulnerability naturally travel along the object's transformation manifold?}
}
]

That wording is quite different from "robust adversarial examples."

---

### There's an even stronger experiment

Compare three things:

**A. Natural transfer**

[
\delta_1
\rightarrow
T(\delta_1)
]

without any transformation-aware optimization.

**B. EOT**

Optimize:

[
\delta_{\rm EOT}
================

\arg\max_\delta E_T[L(f(T(x+\delta)),y)].
]

**C. Independently optimized attacks**

For every transformed image, generate its own:

[
\delta_T^*.
]

Then compare:

[
\underbrace{A\rightarrow T(A)}*{\text{natural}}
\quad
vs
\quad
\underbrace{\text{EOT}}*{\text{optimized}}
\quad
vs
\quad
\underbrace{A_T}_{\text{independent optimum}}.
]

This would tell you something much more fundamental:

> **How much of adversarial vulnerability is tied to the underlying object/representation, and how much is tied to the particular pixel arrangement?**

That's the part I don't see directly answered by the transformation-robust attack literature.

There is even recent work explicitly optimizing transformations to improve adversarial transferability, which reinforces that the field is mostly asking the *optimization* question rather than measuring the natural transformation of a fixed attack. ([ScienceDirect][3])

So I would **not claim "nobody has studied this."** A reviewer could point to EOT and transformation-based transfer attacks immediately.

But I think this narrower question—

> **Given a perturbation optimized at one observation, what is the geometry of its adversarial validity across the object's physical transformation manifold?**

—is sufficiently distinct that it is worth investigating, especially with **iLab-20M**, where you can control the transformation and know exactly which images correspond to the same physical object.

And it connects surprisingly well to your embedding-space idea: you could ask whether **adversarial transfer across transformations correlates with the geometry of the pretrained embedding space**. That could turn this into a study of *representation-level versus pixel-level adversarial vulnerability*, rather than merely another attack paper.
