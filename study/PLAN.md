# Branch `root-cause-study`: what is the root cause of adversarial vulnerability?

Goal: an illustrative, tutorial-style paper (arXiv, for junior researchers and a broad audience) that uses
**simple controlled experiments on toy problems and small datasets** to test, one at a time, the candidate
explanations of adversarial vulnerability, and says plainly which are necessary, which are sufficient, and
which only contribute. Adversarially trained models are now much more robust, yet there is no agreed answer to
"what is the core issue". The paper's value is the *side-by-side, same-yardstick* comparison.

## 1. Organising idea: inevitable vs avoidable

A model is "vulnerable" at budget epsilon when its flip radius is much smaller than epsilon. Two different kinds of reason:

* **Inevitable (data-limited).** Given the data distribution and epsilon, *no* classifier can be both accurate and
  robust (the classes overlap within epsilon, or the evidence is spread so that robustness costs accuracy).
  Our toy world already shows a version of this: the Bayes-optimal rule is as fragile as the network.
* **Avoidable (learning-limited).** A robust, accurate classifier exists, but the data quantity, objective,
  optimiser, or architecture does not find it.

Every hypothesis below is a claim about which of these it explains. This gives the paper a spine and a final scorecard
(hypothesis x {necessary?, sufficient?, inevitable or avoidable?, strength of evidence}).

## 2. Common yardstick (reuse existing code)

* Vulnerability = minimal flip radius per correctly classified point, in l_inf and l_2 (bisection over PGD; exact for linear Bayes rules).
  Code: `root_cause_realdata.py: min_eps`, `root_cause_experiments.py`.
* Always report the pair (l_inf, l_2) and their ratio (a proxy for how "spread" the model's gradient is: l_2/l_inf radius = ||g||_1/||g||_2).
* Always compare against the best possible classifier where the data are generated by us (exact Bayes), and against
  a random-noise baseline (how much random noise of the same size is needed to flip).
* 10 seeds (5 on image data), mean +- s.d.; no clipping on toy tasks; fixed seeds; every number reproducible from `study/`.

## 3. Hypotheses and the experiment that isolates each

| # | Hypothesis (source) | Isolating experiment | What would confirm / refute |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | **Accumulation / linearity** in high dimension (Goodfellow 2015; Simon-Gabriel 2019) | Same evidence spread over k inputs (done); extend to deeper nonlinear nets and to mixed real data (done, MNIST/CIFAR) | l_inf radius ~ 1/sqrt(k) while l_2 flat. Already confirmed on toy, saturated on MNIST. |
| H2 | **Non-robust features**: predictive but small-scale (Ilyas 2019) | Single clue, vary size and reliability independently (done); add the "features, not bugs" test on a toy: train on data containing only small-scale features, check transfer of adversarial examples | Radius tracks size not reliability (done). Transfer test is new. |
| H3 | **Shape vs texture / frequency** (Geirhos 2019; Yin 2019) | Synthetic images where the label is carried by a shape cue (low spatial frequency) and a texture cue (high frequency), each independently controllable in amplitude and reliability; cue-conflict test; attack in pixel and Fourier bases | Prediction to test: vulnerability follows cue *amplitude per pixel x spread*, not cue *category*. If shape-trained nets are no more robust at equal amplitude, "texture" is a proxy, not the cause. |
| H4 | **Sparsity**: (a) sparse vs dense *signal*; (b) weight sparsity; (c) activation sparsity; (d) gradient/saliency sparsity | (a) fixed signal energy in few vs many pixels; (b),(c) done on the toy (weights help, activations do not); (d) relate gradient sparsity (l_1/l_2) to radius across models | Dense signals -> larger ||w||_1/||w||_2 -> l_inf-fragile. Tests whether sparsity of *data* or of the *model* is what matters. |
| H5 | **Off-manifold directions** (Gilmer 2018; Stutz 2019) | Data on a k-dim subspace of d-dim space; attack restricted to tangent vs normal subspace; same on MNIST via PCA | Vulnerability concentrated in normal directions where training gives no signal; fixed by noise augmentation in those directions. |
| H6 | **Not enough data** for robust generalisation (Schmidt 2018) | Gaussian toy and MNIST: vary n and d, adversarially train, compare standard vs robust test accuracy | Robust accuracy needs ~sqrt(d) more data than standard; shows learning-limited component. |
| H7 | **Implicit bias of the optimiser** (gradient descent finds the l_2-max-margin solution, not the l_inf one) | Linear/logistic models on separable toys: gradient descent vs the exact l_inf-max-margin solution (a linear program) at equal accuracy | If the l_inf-max-margin classifier is far more robust at equal accuracy, the *objective/optimiser* (avoidable) is a root cause. |
| H8 | **Noise sensitivity**: adversarial examples are the tail of error under noise (Ford 2019) | Compare random-noise flip radius with adversarial flip radius as d grows | Gap grows like sqrt(d) in l_inf; links accumulation to ordinary test error under noise. |
| H9 | **Architecture / inductive bias** (MLP vs CNN, pooling) | Synthetic images with translation-invariant labels, equal accuracy, vary architecture | Tests whether the right invariances reduce vulnerability or just move it. |
| H10 | **Inevitability from overlap** | Distributions with controlled class overlap at scale epsilon; exact robust Bayes rule vs standard Bayes | Shows when "robust and accurate" is impossible regardless of learning (the Tsipras trade-off), cleanly separating it from H6/H7. |

Secondary / stretch: curvature of decision boundaries (Fawzi 2018), spatial (rotation/shift) robustness (Engstrom 2019),
one-pixel (l_0) attacks vs the weight picture, and a Fourier-basis attack on CIFAR-10.

## 4. Datasets

* Toy Gaussian tasks (exact Bayes available), 2-D visual toys for intuition figures.
* Synthetic images (circles/squares extended with controllable shape/texture/frequency/sparsity cues).
* MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10 (small subsets, small networks) as confirmation, not as the main evidence.

## 5. Pilot result that shaped the plan (`study/pilot_gap.py`)

We asked: does a more robust classifier already "sit in the data"? We compared the trained network's flip radius with the
certified radius of 1-nearest-neighbour on the same training points, (d_other - d_same)/2.

| data | norm | network flip radius (median) | 1-NN certified radius | 1-NN accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MNIST | l_inf | 0.044 | 0.008 | 0.69 |
| MNIST | l_2 | 0.86 | 0.76 | 0.93 |
| CIFAR-10 | l_inf | 0.0029 | 0.008 | 0.13 |
| CIFAR-10 | l_2 | 0.11 | 0.19 | 0.27 |

Lesson: in l_inf on raw pixels, *every* pair of images is almost the same distance apart (nearest other-class and
nearest same-class l_inf distances are both about 0.99 on MNIST), so nearest-neighbour is a useless witness for
"a robust classifier exists". Different-class training points are far apart (0.99 >> 2 x 0.1), so a robust function
exists *on the training set*, but nothing here shows it generalises. So the existence argument only separates
"robust on training points" (easy) from "robust on new points" (H6). The plan therefore treats robust *generalisation* and the
l_inf geometry of the data as first-class questions rather than assuming robust classifiers are easy to find.

## 8. Risks

* Several hypotheses are not mutually exclusive (a shape/texture effect may just be H1 in disguise). The scorecard must say so rather than crown one cause.
* Toy results may not transfer; each hypothesis needs at least one small real-data check before a claim is made.
* "Root cause" is strong language; the paper should claim a map of causes and their relative weight in controlled settings.

## 10. Toy results so far (`study/toy_experiments.py`, `study/toy_figures.py`, `study/results/toy_overview.png`)
* **H10 inevitable ceiling.** In the 1 reliable + 5 weak task the best *linear* rule at eps=0.3 reaches 0.75 accuracy under attack (it uses the reliable clue only);
  the clean-optimal rule and the trained network reach 0.42-0.45. So part of the loss is inevitable (the ceiling falls with eps: 0.63 at eps=0.4) and a large part (about 0.3 at eps=0.3) is
  *avoidable but only by changing the objective*: standard training picks the clean-optimal rule.
* **H4a sparse vs dense signal.** Best-rule flip size falls as 1/sqrt(s) with s informative inputs among 64. The trained network is about 2x more fragile than the best rule when the signal is sparse
  (s=1: 0.22 vs 0.43) and matches it when dense (s=64): the network does not fully ignore irrelevant inputs. Learning-limited component.
* **H8 noise vs worst case.** Ratio (random-noise size)/(worst-case size) grows like ||w||_1/(0.67||w||_2) ~ sqrt(k) (2 at k=4, 11.9 at k=64). No training involved.
* **H7 optimiser bias (n=60 samples, d=100).** With sparse signal the l_inf-max-margin LP beats the l_2-max-margin classifier in both accuracy (0.92 vs 0.86) and flip size (0.57 vs 0.19);
  with dense signal the gap closes. NB: SVM is used as the proxy for the gradient-descent limit; real GD runs still to do.
* **H6 data quantity, standard training.** Flip size equals the best rule's at every n from 30 to 4000 (weight decay 1e-2): in this toy, more data does not change vulnerability.
* **H5 off-manifold.** With weight decay the network ignores off-manifold directions (flip size > 8). Without weight decay off-manifold flip size is 1.3-1.8 vs 1.0 on-manifold, and
  the combined attack is about 20% stronger than on-manifold only: real but modest.
